Image in Paragraph

Before building a lesson, identify what students need to do and where they need support. A polished page is useful only when it helps learners develop the intended skill. Start by asking what successful performance looks like and what currently prevents students from achieving it.

The ADDIE model organizes this work into five connected activities: Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, and Evaluate. For example, if students struggle to troubleshoot a circuit, first investigate the source of the difficulty. They may need help reading a diagram, selecting a measurement, or interpreting the result.

Use what you learn to choose a focused practice activity. A labeled screenshot or diagram can support the explanation, but the paragraph should still communicate the essential idea without requiring students to read tiny text inside the image.

Finally, look for evidence that the activity helped. Review student explanations, observe their decisions, and revise the lesson where confusion remains. Evaluation can inform every stage of the design process.

Essential Skills of an Instructional Designer

Explore each skill area. Choose one strength and one skill you would like to develop next. You can keep more than one menu open to compare them.

▶1. Hands-on skills

Build and test learning materials using tools and workflows that instructors and students actually use. These tasks can be performed with assistive technology or adapted methods.

  • Create a short screencast with clear audio, captions, and a transcript.
  • Build and test a Canvas page, assignment, or practice activity.
  • Prepare demonstration materials with a subject-matter expert; use equipment only with appropriate training.
  • Check the finished activity on a phone and with keyboard navigation.

In practice: Create a two-minute demonstration, then ask a colleague to complete the task using only your instructions.

▶2. Interpersonal skills

Work with people to understand the learning problem and develop a shared solution.

  • Listen actively and ask clarifying questions before proposing a tool.
  • Build trust with instructors and respect their technical expertise.
  • Give specific, constructive feedback and invite feedback on your own work.
  • Facilitate decisions when stakeholders have different priorities.

In practice: An instructor says students are unmotivated. Ask for examples and look together for barriers students encounter.

▶3. Instructional design skills

Connect objectives, practice, and assessment so students rehearse the performance they need.

  • Write measurable objectives tied to an authentic task.
  • Break a complex skill into teachable steps and meaningful practice.
  • Choose examples and feedback that address common misconceptions.
  • Select assessments that demonstrate the intended skill.

In practice: For a troubleshooting objective, design practice in choosing and justifying the next diagnostic test.

▶4. Communication and visual design skills

Make instructions clear, usable, and easy to navigate.

  • Write concise directions with a clear action and expected result.
  • Use headings, spacing, and visuals to organize information.
  • Write useful alternative text and provide captions for media.
  • Use plain language and explain unfamiliar terms.

In practice: Replace a dense paragraph with a short procedure and a labeled visual; keep essential information available as text.

▶5. Analysis and improvement skills

Use evidence to decide what to improve and whether the change helps.

  • Identify performance gaps through observation, questions, and student work.
  • Distinguish a learning need from a process, access, or equipment problem.
  • Review feedback and performance patterns without jumping to conclusions.
  • Revise a lesson and compare evidence of student performance.

In practice: If students miss the same step, review their work and observe the task before adding more content.

Vertical Tabs

Analyze: Understand the Need

Identify the learners, the performance gap, and the conditions that affect learning before choosing a solution.

Example: Interview an instructor to learn why students struggle to troubleshoot a circuit.

Consider: Is the problem missing knowledge, limited practice, unclear instructions, or something outside instruction?

Design: Align the Learning

Define measurable objectives, decide what evidence will demonstrate success, and plan activities that prepare learners for the assessment.

Example: Plan guided troubleshooting practice and a skills assessment that requires students to locate a circuit fault.

Consider: Does the assessment measure the same skill named in the objective?

Develop: Build and Test

Create the materials and activities. Check accuracy, accessibility, clarity, and usability.

Example: Build a Canvas lesson with a captioned demonstration, an accessible diagram, and guided practice.

Consider: Can learners use the materials with a keyboard and assistive technology?

Implement: Support the Experience

Prepare instructors and learners, confirm that resources work, and provide support as instruction begins.

Example: Introduce the Canvas lesson, explain the lab expectations, and confirm that students can access the demonstration.

Consider: What preparation will help instructors and students use this lesson successfully?

Evaluate: Improve with Evidence

Evaluate throughout the design process. Gather evidence about learning and performance, then use it to improve instruction. Rather than treating evaluation as a final step, build quick checks into every phase—from needs analysis and storyboarding to prototyping and pilot testing. Catching misaligned objectives or confusing navigation early keeps revisions fast and inexpensive.

Example: Review troubleshooting errors and student feedback to identify where clearer practice is needed.

Consider: What demonstrates improved performance beyond students saying they liked the lesson?

Flip & Answer Cards

Flip Card Carousel

Retrieval Practice What does this mean?

Actively recall information from memory before checking the answer. For example, explain a concept without looking at your notes, then check your explanation.

Where does backward design begin? Predict the first step.

Begin with the desired learning outcomes. Determine what evidence will demonstrate success, then plan activities that prepare learners to produce that evidence.

Formative Assessment What makes an assessment formative?

Evidence gathered during learning is used to guide improvement. For example, a brief knowledge check helps an instructor identify a misconception and adjust the next activity.

Accessible Design How can you reduce barriers to learning?

Design materials that learners can perceive, navigate, and use in different ways. For example, provide accurate captions for a demonstration and meaningful text alternatives for essential diagrams.

Scaffolding What support helps a beginner succeed?

Provide temporary support, such as a worked example, checklist, or instructor coaching. Gradually remove that support as learners become more independent.

Cognitive Load How can you reduce unnecessary mental effort?

Remove distracting details, organize instructions into clear steps, and place related information together. Keep attention on the decisions and skills learners need to practice.

Learning Alignment How do objectives, practice, and assessment connect?

Use the same target skill across the objective, learning activity, and assessment. If students must troubleshoot a fault, give them troubleshooting practice and assess their troubleshooting performance.

Spaced Practice Why revisit a skill across several sessions?

Distribute practice over time instead of completing it all at once. Revisiting a skill after a delay gives learners another opportunity to retrieve and strengthen their learning.

SALT ELEMENTS • COMPONENT GUIDE

Component Guide

Type a word such as image, practice, or lab to filter the list.

Component Selection Guide

SALT components: best use and when to avoid them
Component Best Use Avoid When
Banner, lead & labels Orient learners and establish hierarchy. They repeat the same title or introduction.
Learning objective cards State observable learning outcomes. They merely list topics.
Callout Emphasize one key principle or warning. Every paragraph competes for attention.
Fact cards Group short related concepts. The ideas require extended explanation.
Comparison cards Contrast a few approaches. There is no meaningful distinction.
Image in a paragraph Wrap explanatory text around a small screenshot or diagram that supports the same idea. On smaller screens, the image stacks above the text. The image contains small labels or detailed instructions that require a full-width view, or the image adds no useful information to the paragraph.
Standalone image / lightbox Provide a shared visual reference with optional enlargement and a useful caption. The image adds no useful information or enlargement is needed to read basic content.
Image cards Connect images with names, functions, and distinguishing details. Add data-caption-lines to the grid to show more or fewer caption lines before “Read more.” Images are unnecessary or explanations become lengthy.
Image carousel Browse a related set of images with captions and optional enlargement. Learners must compare all images at once or follow essential steps that should remain visible.
Video container / Canvas Studio embed Demonstrate a process or introduce a perspective with a focused viewing prompt. A brief explanation or image communicates the idea more clearly, or accessible media alternatives are missing.
Expandable reveal Offer a hint, reflection prompt, or extra explanation. Groups can include an Open all / Close all button. Essential directions or safety information must be immediately visible.
Comparison table Compare consistent attributes across items. A simple list is clearer.
Horizontal tabs Explore a few related categories with short labels. Learners must compare panels side by side or read every panel in a required sequence.
Vertical tabs Organize related topics with longer labels beside a shared content panel. The content needs side-by-side comparison or the tabs would hide essential instructions.
Dialog box Offer optional supporting detail without leaving the page. Essential instructions would be hidden or the content needs an extended reading space.
Table term reveal Recall terms from definitions before revealing each answer; a counter tracks progress and Hide all supports another round of review. Learners need an always-visible glossary.
Paragraph term reveal Recall a concept within a meaningful sentence or paragraph. Hidden words make the passage confusing.
Flip card carousel Practice recalling concise concepts one card at a time; shuffle for varied review. Long text, essential directions, or side-by-side comparison is needed.
Answer question block & answer cards Present a clear question, choose an answer mentally, and reveal feedback to compare reasoning. A recorded response or grade is required, or revealing feedback would replace the intended practice.
Name that part Recall the names of numbered parts in a labeled image or diagram, then flip each card to check. The numbers are hard to read in the image, or parts must be identified in a required order.
Image identification / scenario game Distinguish pictured items or use a clear scenario to select an answer and review feedback. Use data-question to change the question for scenario games. Visual differences are unclear, choices are ambiguous, or scores must be recorded in Canvas.
Sorting game Classify terms or examples into meaningful categories using drag, dropdown, or keyboard controls. Items reasonably belong in more than one category or the task requires ordering rather than classification.
Lab prep checklist Help students confirm readiness before a lab, clinical, or shop day. Progress is saved in the student’s browser. Completion must be tracked by the instructor, or the list contains safety steps that require sign-off.
Knowledge check / scenario Practice a decision with immediate feedback. A score must be recorded in the gradebook.
Component Guide Choose a component by its instructional purpose and limitations. It substitutes for trying the component and checking the student experience.
Key takeaway / achievement cards Reinforce a few central ideas at the end of a lesson. They introduce essential new content, reproduce the lesson, or are treated as proof of mastery.
Completion statement / next step Signal the end of the page and explain the next action. It implies that viewing the page or its animation automatically records Canvas completion.
Sidebar & resource links Navigate major sections in page order and reach supporting references. A short page needs no extra navigation.
Progress bar & back to top Support orientation and navigation on a long page. Scroll progress is treated as evidence of learning or recorded completion.
